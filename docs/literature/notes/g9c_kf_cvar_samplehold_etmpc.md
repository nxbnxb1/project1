### KC1 — Risk-Aware Control of Discrete-Time Stochastic Systems: Integrating Kalman Filter and Worst-case CVaR in Control Barrier Functions
Masako Kishida, Senior Member, IEEE (National Institute of Informatics, Tokyo). arXiv:2312.15638v4 [math.OC], 18 Dec 2024 (arXiv stamp on p.1). No venue, journal, conference or DOI is printed anywhere in the PDF. Funding footnote: JST PRESTO Grant JPMJPR22C3. Pages read: 6/6.
Status: USE (narrow) — the only paper of the three that turns Kalman-filter mean/covariance into a tightened (risk-aware) CBF constraint, which is the closest formal analogue of our rho + d_s + beta*sigma inflation; but it is a linear-system, single-step-CBF, toy-example paper and gives no probability-of-safety certificate either.

**Problem & setting:**
* Discrete-time linear systems with stochastic disturbances and partial state measurement — p.1: "This paper proposes control approaches for discrete-time linear systems subject to stochastic disturbances."
* Pipeline: KF → worst-case CVaR → CBF constraint — p.1: "It employs Kalman filter to estimate the mean and covariance of the state propagation, and the worst-case conditional value-at-risk (CVaR) to quantify the tail risk using the estimated mean and covariance." and p.1: "The quantified risk is then integrated into a control barrier function (CBF) to derive constraints for controller synthesis, addressing tail risks near safe set boundaries."
* Gap claimed — p.1: "although many existing approaches assume that the all the system states can be observed, which may be impractical in some real applications" and p.1: "Yet, comprehensive solutions addressing both stochastic disturbances and unmeasured states remain limited".
* Only two safe-set shapes are treated — p.1: "Two optimization-based control methods are presented using the obtained constraints for half-space and ellipsoidal safe sets, respectively."
* Model (eq. 5, p.2): x_{t+1} = A x_t + B u_t + w_t, z_t = H x_t + v_t, constant matrices. Noise assumptions (p.2–3): w_t, v_t i.i.d., zero mean, finite covariances Q, R, mutually independent with x_0 — p.3: "the disturbance wt are independent and identically distributed random vectors with E[wt] = 0 and finite covariance". No Gaussianity is assumed — p.3: "The Kalman filter, a recursive estimator, optimal for linear systems with Gaussian noise, is adapted here for disturbances and noise, which are not necessarily Gaussians."

**Method:**
* **Worst-case CVaR (Sec. II-B, p.1–2).** Ambiguity set = all distributions with the given mean and covariance (p.1). CVaR definition (Def. 2.1, p.2) — p.2: "CVaR is the conditional expectation of losses exceeding the ... quantifying the tail risk of loss function". Worst-case CVaR = supremum of CVaR over the ambiguity set (Def. 2.2). It is coherent (Prop. 2.1: sub-additivity, positive homogeneity, monotonicity, translation invariance). For quadratic losses it is an SDP — p.2: "the worst-case CVaR can be computed via a semidefinite program" (Lemma 2.1). Lemma 2.2 (p.2) bounds the worst-case CVaR of a zero-mean linear loss qᵀξ by |q|ᵀ times the element-wise worst-case CVaR of ξ.
* **Kalman filter (Sec. III-B, p.3).** Standard prediction/update. Prediction: mean x̄_{t+1|t} = A x̄_{t|t} + B u_t, covariance P_{t+1|t} = A P_{t|t} Aᵀ + Q (read from page image p.3) — p.3: "In the prediction phase, the filter forecasts the next state and its uncertainty." Update with gain K_{t+1}; the Kalman gain (eq. 23, p.4) is chosen because it minimises the posterior covariance, which in turn minimises the CBF left-hand side — p.4: "By looking back Lemma 2.1, this left-hand-side is minimized when Ω, or Σ is small. Consequently, we employ the gain Kt+1, known as the Kalman gain, which minimizes the error covariance".
* **Risk-aware CBF (Def. 3.1, p.3).** x̂_{t|t} is a random vector with KF mean and covariance; x̂_{t+1|t} = A x̂_{t|t} + B u_t + w_t (eq. 6). ξ_{t|t} stacks x̂_{t|t} and w_t; its centred version has covariance blockdiag(P_{t|t}, Q) (eqs. 7–8). The condition (eq. 9) is: worst-case CVaR_ε[ −h(x̂_{t+1|t}) + α h(x̂_{t|t}) ] ≤ 0, i.e. the discrete-time CBF inequality h(x_{t+1}) ≥ α h(x_t) (Def. 2.3, eq. 4, p.2) is imposed on the ε-tail instead of the mean — p.3: "This subsection integrates Kalman filter and worst-case CVaR to introduce Kalman filter-based risk-aware discrete-time CBF."
* **Half-space safe set (Thm 4.1, p.3).** For h(x) = qᵀx + r, (9) holds iff −qᵀB u_t ≤ φ(x̂_{t|t}), where φ = − (worst-case CVaR_ε of −qᵀ[A − αI, I] ξᶜ_{t|t}) + qᵀ(A − αI) x̄_{t|t} + (1 − α) r (eq. 12, checked on page image p.3) — p.3: "This scenario allows us to reformulate the constraint (9) as a linear function of the control input u". In words (our inference, from eq. 12): the right-hand side equals the mean-based discrete CBF bound minus a back-off term that depends only on P_{t|t}, Q, ε, α, q and A (not on u) — a covariance-dependent constraint tightening.
* **Ellipsoidal safe set (Prop. 4.1, Thm 4.2, p.4).** h(x) = −(x − x_c)ᵀE(x − x_c) + r, i.e. the state must stay INSIDE an ellipsoid. Exact condition (16) is an SDP-type check — p.4: "Although checking the satisfaction of (16) for a given ut is straightforward, characterizing the exact set of ut that satisfies (16) is not except for some simple cases." A sufficient condition quadratic in u, with an auxiliary variable v_t bounding |u_t|, is given (eqs. 18–19) — p.4: "a sufficient condition for the constraint (9) to be satisfied can be expressed using a quadratic function of the control input u".
* **Controllers (Sec. V, p.4–5).** Method 1: min ‖u − u_nom‖² subject to the risk-aware CBF constraint (eq. 25) — p.4: "to adapt a nominal controller unom(x), which does not account for safety constraints, to comply with derived safety conditions by minimally modifying it". If infeasible, a penalised slack δ is added (eq. 26) — p.5: "If infeasible, a modified optimization problem incorporating a penalty parameter ρ > 0 to balance safety and controller deviation can be used." Method 2: CLF-CBF optimisation (eq. 29) with a relaxed CLF constraint on the KF mean.

**Quantitative results:**
* Only one example (Sec. VI, p.5): 1-D vehicle, position + velocity, acceleration input, position measured — p.5: "With the sampling time 0.05, the system’s dynamics are modeled as follows". Q given in eq. (31), R = 0.09; half-space safe set h(x) = [0.4 0.4]x + 1 ≥ 0 (eq. 32, a combined position–velocity constraint); p.5: "The design parameters ε = 0.3 and α = 0.7"; x̄_0 = [7 0]ᵀ, P_0 = Q; nominal u = −15x_1 − 5x_2 (eq. 33); simulated for time 4.
* Baselines (p.5): "Ignore constraint (w = 0)" and a controller that uses "the expected value instead of the worst-case CVaR (Expected value-based)".
* Fig. 1(a)/(b), p.6 (phase portraits; "Fig. 1: Phase portrait for vehicle navigation: the red regions indicate the unsafe region h(x) < 0"). Outcome stated in text, p.5: "We observe in both figures that ignoring safety constraints leads to trajectories entering unsafe regions even without disturbances." and p.5: "the most part of the trajectories of expected value-based controller enters the unsafe region while resulting that of the proposed controller remain in the safe region regardless of the stochastic disturbance." and p.5: "This shows the use of expected value is not sufficient to remain in the safe region." KF tracking — p.5: "The Kalman filter’s estimation, which are used to obtain the control inputs, align closely with true dynamics for both the proposed and expected value-based controllers."
* No table, no number of Monte-Carlo runs, no violation rate, no margin/cost numbers: Fig. 1 shows one trajectory per controller (read from the figure). So the evidence is qualitative.

**Limitations stated by the authors:**
* Only linear systems; nonlinear extension and other observers are future work — p.6: "Future research directions include investigating the applicability of other state observers within this framework to potentially broaden the approach’s versatility, as well as expanding these techniques to nonlinear systems".
* Ellipsoidal case only sufficient — p.4 quote above ("characterizing the exact set of ut that satisfies (16) is not except for some simple cases").
* Possible infeasibility of Method 1 handled by a penalty/slack (p.5 quote above).

**Citable statements:**
1. Bộ lọc Kalman cung cấp trung bình và hiệp phương sai của trạng thái, và có thể dùng chúng để định lượng rủi ro đuôi rồi đưa vào ràng buộc CBF — p.1: "It employs Kalman filter to estimate the mean and covariance of the state propagation, and the worst-case conditional value-at-risk (CVaR) to quantify the tail risk using the estimated mean and covariance."
2. Với tập an toàn nửa không gian (h affine), ràng buộc CBF có xét rủi ro (KF + worst-case CVaR) vẫn là một ràng buộc tuyến tính theo đầu vào điều khiển — p.3: "This scenario allows us to reformulate the constraint (9) as a linear function of the control input u".
3. Chỉ dùng giá trị kỳ vọng của ước lượng (bỏ qua hiệp phương sai) là không đủ để giữ an toàn trong ví dụ mô phỏng của tác giả — p.5: "This shows the use of expected value is not sufficient to remain in the safe region."
4. Hiệp phương sai sai số nhỏ hơn làm ràng buộc an toàn ít chặt hơn; vì vậy tác giả dùng độ lợi Kalman (cực tiểu hiệp phương sai sau cập nhật) — p.4: "this left-hand-side is minimized when Ω, or Σ is small. Consequently, we employ the gain Kt+1, known as the Kalman gain, which minimizes the error covariance".
5. Các phương pháp CBF xét đồng thời nhiễu ngẫu nhiên và trạng thái không đo được trực tiếp vẫn còn ít — p.1: "Yet, comprehensive solutions addressing both stochastic disturbances and unmeasured states remain limited".
6. Bộ lọc Kalman ở đây được dùng cả khi nhiễu không phải Gauss (chỉ cần trung bình và hiệp phương sai hữu hạn) — p.3: "is adapted here for disturbances and noise, which are not necessarily Gaussians".

**Relation to our method:**
* **Where to cite:** Related work (risk-aware / uncertainty-aware CBFs using state estimates) and Method/Discussion when we justify inflating obstacle constraints with the KF standard deviation (claim (a)).
* **Same:** (i) a Kalman filter supplies mean and covariance, and the CBF constraint is evaluated on the mean but tightened by a term that grows with the covariance; with an affine h the tightened constraint stays linear in u, so it fits a QP (our inference: Kishida's Method 1 with eq. (11) is a QP, though the paper does not use the word). (ii) Between measurements the KF prediction step P_{t+1|t} = A P_{t|t} Aᵀ + Q grows the covariance, the same mechanism that grows our position covariance between frames (our inference; Kishida measures at every step, so this growth only happens for one step there).
* **Different:** (i) Kishida derives the back-off from a worst-case CVaR at level ε over all distributions with the KF moments; our beta*sigma with beta = 2 is a fixed heuristic multiple of sigma and we claim no probability or CVaR level. (ii) Kishida's safe sets are a half-space or the inside of an ellipsoid; our obstacles are the outside of vertical cylinders (non-convex), so the paper's results apply to us only after the cylinder constraint is linearised into a half-space per step (our inference). (iii) Linear time-invariant model with measurement every step; ours has event-triggered, latent (~0.1 s) measurements and an MPC + 100 Hz filter. (iv) Single-step CBF in a QP/CLF-CBF, not an MPC with CBF rows over a horizon.
* **Possible bridge (our inference, NOT printed in the paper — must be checked against Kishida's ref. [22], Zymler et al. 2013, before any use):** for a zero-mean linear loss the worst-case CVaR over the mean–covariance ambiguity set is known to equal sqrt((1−ε)/ε)·sqrt(qᵀΣq), i.e. a multiple of the standard deviation along q. If that holds, Kishida's half-space back-off reduces to (multiple)·sigma, and our beta = 2 would correspond to ε = 0.2 for a one-step, linearised constraint. Kishida itself only states the SDP form (Lemma 2.1, p.2), so we must not attribute the closed form to this paper.
* **Draft wording for our paper (our own sentence, not a quote):** _Similar covariance-based tightening of CBF constraints has been derived from Kalman-filter moments and worst-case CVaR for linear systems [KC1]; our inflation beta*sigma follows the same structure but is a deterministic heuristic without a risk certificate._

**Caveats:**
* arXiv preprint v4; no venue printed. Single author. Toy 1-D example, qualitative figure only (one run per controller), no statistics.
* No theorem gives a probability of remaining safe over time; the guarantee is the per-step CVaR condition (Def. 3.1). The sentence on p.2 "The existence of a CBF guarantees that the control system is safe assuming that the initial state is in C" refers to the deterministic CBF (Def. 2.3).
* Notation slip in the paper: the CLF weight is Φ in eq. (27) but Ψ in eq. (34) (p.5, page image).
* The ellipsoidal case keeps the state inside an ellipsoid, i.e. it is not an obstacle-avoidance constraint.

### SH1 — Sample-and-Hold Safety with Control Barrier Functions
Gilbert Bahati, Pio Ong, and Aaron D. Ames (Department of Mechanical and Civil Engineering, California Institute of Technology). arXiv:2304.08685v2 [eess.SY], 3 Oct 2023 (arXiv stamp on p.1). No venue, conference, journal or DOI is printed anywhere in the PDF. Funding footnote: NSF CPS Award #1932091 and the Technology Innovation Institute (TII). Pages read: 8/8.
Status: USE — directly addresses claim (b): it shows that a CBF safety filter implemented with zero-order hold violates safety at any finite sampling rate, formalises the margin (set expansion d) that a given sampling time needs, and gives a violation-free controller adjustment and an event-triggered sampling rule; it is the right reference for the missing guarantee of our 100 Hz filter.

**Problem & setting:**
* The assumption being examined — p.1: "A common assumption on the deployment of safeguarding controllers on the digital platform is that high sampling frequency translates to a small violation of safety."
* Source of the error — p.1: "However, this can result in measurement uncertainties due to sampling (i.e., zero-order hold errors), which can lead to safety violations." and p.1: "However, this does not entirely eliminate the error, only reducing it to an acceptable level."
* Discrete-time designs do not cover the hold interval — p.1: "discrete time designs explicitly guarantee safety only at sampling instants (when the controller is updated) and not in the inter-sample periods (when the control signal is being held constant) and as such, safety may be violated during this period."
* Prior event-triggered CBF work expands the safe set — p.1: "But these instantiations required expansion of the safe set to accommodate inter-sample errors."
* Contribution — p.1: "The main contribution of this paper is a robust control framework for sample-and-hold control systems that establishes safety without violations or relaxations of the safe set of interest."
* Setting: continuous-time, control-affine ẋ = f(x) + g(x)u (eq. 7, p.2) with a continuous-time CBF condition ḣ ≥ −α(h) (eq. 3) — p.2: "We focus on discretely instantiated continuous-time systems that result from sample-and-hold (i.e., zero-order hold) implementations of safeguarding controllers on digital platforms". The input k_nom(x(t_i)) "is held constant until ti+1, the next sampling instant" (p.2); the closed loop is ẋ = f(x) + g(x)k_nom(x + e) with sample-and-hold error e = x(t_i) − x (eq. 8, p.2–3).

**Method:**
* **Why the nominal CBF controller fails (Sec. III-B, p.3).** Under hold, ḣ = L_f h(x) + L_g h(x) k_nom(x + e) (eq. 9) may violate ḣ ≥ −α(h) — p.3: "Thus, safety of the sample-and-hold system on C cannot be guaranteed without an additional assumption accounting for sample-and-hold errors, which is why practical safety considers allowing minor safety violations."
* **Assumptions (p.3–6):** A1 f, g and controller Lipschitz ("The functions f, g and the controller k are Lipschitz continuous", p.3); A2 ‖L_g h‖ ≤ λ on C; A3 L_g h Lipschitz (constant M); A4 proximal level curves of h; A5 bounded dynamics ‖ẋ‖ ≤ B_e; A6 ‖L_g h‖ ≥ µ > 0 on ∂C; A7 h ≥ h_min > 0 at distance ≥ δ from ∂C. Bounds are needed only on the region of operation — p.3: "if the final region of operation is compact, then all bounds exist, and Lipschitzness only needs to be local."
* **ISSf form (eq. 12, p.3):** ḣ ≥ −α(h) − L_k λ ‖e‖, with L_k the controller's Lipschitz constant (read from page image p.3).
* **Practical safety formalised.** Expanded set D = {h + d ≥ 0} (eq. 13) — p.3: "for some positive constant d > 0 dictating the size of safety violation being accommodated." Thm 1 (p.4): "there exists an upper bound emax > 0 on the sample-and-hold error" such that ‖e‖∞ ≤ e_max makes D forward invariant, with e_max = −α(−d)/(L_k λ′) (proof, p.4). Lemma 1 (p.4): ‖e(t)‖ ≤ B′_e T_s within a hold interval (the state moves at most at the bounded speed). Thm 2 (p.5): "for each d > 0, there exists a sampling time Ts > 0 small enough such that the expanded set D given in (13) is forward invariant", with the explicit bound T_s ≤ −α(−d)/(L_k λ′ B′_e) (proof, p.5, read from page image).
* **Violation-free adjustment (Sec. IV, eq. 15, p.5):** k(x) = k_nom(x) + (1/ε) L_g h(x)ᵀ — p.5: "Our strategy introduces a robustness term (without expanding the safe set) in the ISSf-BC to deal with the sample-and-hold error." The term "adjusts a nominal controller in the direction that increases the barrier function h, which will provide robustness against sample-and-hold errors." Thm 3 (p.5–6): T_s < µ′/(B(εL_k + M)) gives ḣ ≥ −α(h) near ∂C, hence invariance of the original C (Nagumo). Thm 4 (p.6): splitting α into α′ = (1 + c)α and using h ≥ h_min away from the boundary gives a barrier condition on all of C with T_s < min{cα(h_min)/(λ(L_k + M/ε)), µ′/(B(εL_k + M))} (eq. 21, page image p.6).
* **Event-triggered sampling (Sec. IV-C, p.6–7):** trigger function T(x, e) = ḣ(x, k(x + e)) + α′(h(x)) (eq. 22); resample when it reaches 0 (eq. 23) — p.6: "In this framework, the control is sampled at time instants (i.e., events) prescribed by a state-based criterion, rather than periodically based on time." Thm 5 (p.7): "Under Assumptions 1-3 and 5-7, there exists a minimum inter-event time" — so no Zeno behaviour and C is forward invariant. Tuning — p.7: "small values of these parameters will result in a higher sampling rate while large values will result in lack of robustness or larger control input, respectively."

**Quantitative results (adaptive cruise control, Sec. V, p.7–8):**
* Model (p.7): 3-state ACC with m = 1650 kg, v_0 = 13.89 m/s, f_0 = 0.1 N, f_1 = 5 Ns/m, f_2 = 0.25 Ns²/m (read from page image p.7); desired speed v_d = 22 m/s; barrier h(x) = x_3 − T_h(x)x_2 with T_h = 1.8x_2; nominal k_nom is a CBF-QP safety filter (p.7: "To ensure safety, we implement a Quadratic Program (QP) based safety controller that minimally adjusts the desired control input udes through a safety filter").
* Fig. 1, p.3 (initialised at h = 0) — p.3: "Safety under the nominal controller via sample-and-hold. Here, we see violations regardless of the sampling frequency". (a) k_nom at 10, 20, 30, 40, 50 Hz: all curves dip below h = 0; the deepest dip is about −2×10⁻⁴ (read from the figure). (b) adjusted controller with ε = 1 at 1, 1.1, 1.3, 1.5, 2.5 Hz; the caption says "Here, we satisfy safety even for slow sampling frequencies.", but red dotted violation segments down to about −6×10⁻⁴ are still drawn for at least one of the slowest rates (read from the figure; I cannot assign them to a specific rate).
* Fig. 2, p.7 (initialised at positive h; parameters p.7: "with c = 9.18, hmin = 0.0005, and ε = 2.5"). (a) k_nom at 2, 3, 4, 5, 10 Hz: all violate, down to about −1×10⁻³ (read from the figure). (b) adjusted controller: only the 2 Hz curve violates (about −0.25×10⁻³); 3–10 Hz stay ≥ 0 (read from the figure). Text, p.7: "It can be seen that slower sampling frequencies result in safety violations." and "by utilizing Theorems 3 and 4, we are able to achieve safety for the original safe set C at fast enough sampling frequencies."
* Fig. 3, p.8 (event-triggered): "Event Triggered Control results showing (from left to right): barrier function values, trigger condition satisfaction, and sampled control values." h stays ≥ 0 (rising to ≈3.4×10⁻³ by 5.5 s); about 7 control samples in the first ≈4.5 s with growing inter-event times (≈0.4 s up to ≈1.3 s); wheel force ≈60.25 → 59.9 N (all read from the figure). Text, p.7–8: "we leverage Theorem 5 to demonstrate how the event-triggered control strategy ... achieves less conservative sampling while still ensuring the safety of our original safe set". The values of c, ε for the event-triggered run are not stated.
* No tables; no hardware.

**Limitations stated by the authors:**
* Conservative, hard-to-compute bounds — p.6: "The main drawback of Theorem 4 is its conservatism in the sampling time it guarantees." and p.6: "we elect to omit the bound on the safe sampling time ... which are difficult to find in practice." and p.6: "In addition, our derivations are overly conservative at some key steps." Example — p.6: "the bound on sample-and-hold error in Lemma 1 supposes the state deviates as fast as possible from the state where it is last sampled."
* Thm 3 gives no barrier condition inside C — p.6: "However, this result does not provide any barrier condition (3) for every state x in the safe set C."
* The adjustment itself is held too — p.5: "However, the newly added term itself will also suffer from errors due to sample-and-hold, and so careful analysis is needed to assess the full benefit of the adjustment."
* Needs control authority on the boundary — p.5: "For states x where Lgh(x) = 0, the barrier function h evolves at the mercy of the drift f, as there is no way of influencing it with any control input."
* Measurement errors not treated — p.8: "Future work involves the extension of our work to study the effects of measurement errors associated with the sampling of safeguarding controllers."
* The ISSf bound of Thm 1 is not uniform in d — p.4: "Fortunately, we avoid arbitrarily large sample-and-hold errors through high frequency sampling, which we discuss next."

**Citable statements:**

Thường người ta giả định rằng lấy mẫu với tần số cao chỉ gây vi phạm an toàn nhỏ khi triển khai bộ điều khiển an toàn trên nền tảng số — p.1: "A common assumption on the deployment of safeguarding controllers on the digital platform is that high sampling frequency translates to a small violation of safety."

### ET1 — Event-triggered and distributed model predictive control for guaranteed collision avoidance in UAV swarms
Alexander Gräfe, Joram Eickhoff, Sebastian Trimpe (Institute for Data Science in Mechanical Engineering (DSME), RWTH Aachen University). p.1: "Accepted for publication at the IFAC Conference on Networked Systems (NecSys) 2022" and "This work has been accepted to IFAC for publication under a Creative Commons Licence CC-BY-NC-ND." arXiv:2206.11020v1 [eess.SY], 22 Jun 2022 (stamp on p.1). No DOI, volume or page numbers printed. Keywords (p.2): Distributed constrained control and MPC, Event-triggered and self-triggered control, Multi-agent systems. Funding: DFG priority program 1914 (grant TR 1433/1-2). Pages read: 7/7.
Status: USE (narrow) — the event trigger here schedules which UAVs get their MPC replanned on a limited number of ground computers (no perception, no static obstacles), so it is only a contrast for our event-triggered sensing; its terminal-stop recursive-feasibility argument and its inter-sample distance margin are, however, directly parallel to our terminal stop constraint and our discrete CBF rows.

**Problem & setting:**
* DMPC for swarms — p.2: "Distributed model predictive control (DMPC) is often used to tackle path planning for unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV) swarms." On-board cost — p.2: "However, the on-board processing needs computational capacity and thus increases weight, leading to a higher energy consumption of the UAVs."
* Idea — p.2: "In this work, we propose to offload path planning computations to multiple ground-based computation units." and p.2: "As simultaneously communicating and recomputing all trajectories is not feasible for a large swarm with tight timing requirements, we develop a novel event-triggered DMPC that selects a subset of most relevant UAV trajectories to be replanned."
* M computation units (CUs) for N ≥ M UAVs — p.2: "We thus propose to use fewer CUs than UAVs and present an event-triggering (ET) mechanism that enables trajectory replanning for only a subset of UAVs at a time." p.4: "Every CU can only replan the path of one UAV in one round and every CU should replan the path of a different UAV."
* Task: reach targets without inter-UAV collisions, distance in a scaled metric ‖Θ⁻¹(p_i − p_j)‖ ≥ r_min (eq. 2, p.4) — p.4: "Because some types of UAV need to maintain a higher distance in one direction, e.g. due to downwash, the coordinate system is scaled using a symmetric positive definite matrix".
* Assumptions — p.3: "In this work, we assume a perfect connection without any message loss. However, we assume that the network has limited bandwidth and some delay." and p.3: "we assume that the dynamics of the UAVs can be modeled by a linear system. We also assume that they are able to hover". Feasibility is part of safety — p.4: "Additionally, the whole system has to guarantee that the DMPC problem is feasible at all time. Otherwise, the UAVs might collide because the algorithm fails to find a trajectory."

**Method:**
* **Round-based many-to-all network (p.4):** each round = communication (T_com) + scheduling/replanning (T_calc); round length T = T_calc + T_com — p.4: "The combined duration between the starts of two rounds is T = Tcalc + Tcom." All CUs have the same information and pick distinct UAVs.
* **MPC (eq. 4, p.4–5):** piecewise-constant input — p.4: "The control input for each UAV is piecewise-constant with sampling time Ts and horizon hs". Quadratic cost to target; exact discretisation of the linear model (4b); input/state bounds (4c–d); terminal constraint v_i = 0, y_i = 0 at the horizon end HT (4e) — p.5: "Equation (4e) is important for the feasibility at the next sample time point" and p.5: "Thus, at the end of the horizon, the UAV stops and stays at its position". Delay handled by starting the new plan one round later — p.5: "Because of the delay of calculation and communication, the trajectory starts in the next step (k + 1)T."
* **Collision constraint (4f), eq. (5), p.5:** time-variant buffered Voronoi cells (TV-BVC) built from the other UAVs' last planned trajectories, at constraint sample times hT_c: a separating plane in the middle, with distance at least 0.5·r̂_min on each side. Motivation — p.3: "However, the anti-collision constraints presented in Luis and Schoellig (2019) do not guarantee collision free trajectories, because the pairwise constraints have common intersections".
* **If not triggered:** the UAV keeps following its previous plan (eqs. 6–7) — p.5: "the UAV reuses the previously planned trajectory similar to Cai et al. (2018)" and p.5: "In both situations, replanning or reusing, the resulting trajectories are not closer than".
* **Guarantees:** Lemma 1 and Thm 1 (p.5): recursive feasibility and separation ≥ r̂_min at the constraint sample points. Inter-sample gap — p.5: "However, this does not guarantee that (2) is fulfilled and the UAVs do not collide. The trajectories might get too close in between the sample time points (Luis and Schoellig (2019))." Thm 2 (p.6): if r̂_min − Δp_i,max(T_c) − Δp_j,max(T_c) ≥ r_min, where Δp_max(T̃) bounds how far a UAV can move "as upper bounds of the maximum distance, the position can travel between two sample time points with a sample time" (p.6), then (2) holds for all t (read from page image p.6). Independence from the trigger — p.6: "Theorem 2 is independent of the chosen ET." and p.6: "Second, each UAV can recursively generate collision-free trajectories with (6) without triggering. However, when a UAV’s replanning is not triggered for H consecutive times, it will stop and stay at a position which might not be its target position."
* **Trigger = priority-based (PBT), eq. (9), p.6:** priority g_i = α₁·(distance to target) + α₂·Δt_i (time since last replanning, p.6: "describes how long the trajectory of UAV i was not replanned") − α₃·(a term counting UAVs inside a cone of angle ±β towards the target, weighted by closeness). p.6: "CU q then replans the trajectory of the UAV with q-th highest priority." The trigger is chosen for performance, not safety — p.6: "The choice of the ET is thus important to quickly steer the UAVs into their targets."

**Quantitative results:**
